*I spent two years training models on peptides before I could explain, out loud, what a peptide
actually is. This post is the explanation I wish someone had given me on day one, and the first
in a series on machine learning for biology.*

---

## 1. Twenty letters, one alphabet 🔤

Life writes with twenty letters.

That's it. Every protein in every living thing, from the enzyme digesting your lunch to the
keratin in your hair to the antibodies hunting a virus, is a **string** built from twenty
**amino acids**.

Each amino acid gets a one-letter code:

```
A  Alanine        M  Methionine
C  Cysteine       N  Asparagine
D  Aspartic acid  P  Proline
E  Glutamic acid  Q  Glutamine
F  Phenylalanine  R  Arginine
G  Glycine        S  Serine
H  Histidine      T  Threonine
I  Isoleucine     V  Valine
K  Lysine         W  Tryptophan
L  Leucine        Y  Tyrosine
```

So a real biological molecule can be written down like this:

```
GLFDIIKKIAESF
```

Thirteen letters. That's a peptide. If you're a programmer, your instinct is already correct:
**this is a string, and strings are something we know how to model.**

---

## 2. Word, sentence, novel 📖

Here's the distinction that confused me longest, so let's kill it immediately.

| Name | Length | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Amino acid** | 1 residue | a letter |
| **Peptide** | ~2–50 residues | a word or short sentence |
| **Polypeptide** | ~50+ residues | a paragraph |
| **Protein** | usually 100+, folded, functional | a whole chapter, bound into a book |

There is no hard border between "long peptide" and "small protein". Biology doesn't care about
our taxonomies. The practical rule most papers use: **under about 50 residues, call it a
peptide.**

And that length difference is not cosmetic. It changes everything downstream:

- A protein has enough length to fold into a big stable 3-D shape with pockets and grooves.
- A peptide is short, floppy, and often only takes a definite shape *when it touches its target*.

Which is why, when you build models, **peptide prediction is a genuinely different problem
from protein prediction.** Tools tuned for 300-residue proteins routinely fall over on
15-residue peptides. I learned this the expensive way.

---

## 3. The chain: how letters get joined ⛓️

Every amino acid has the same skeleton and one variable part:

```
        H   R          <- R is the "side chain": this is what differs
        │   │             between the 20 amino acids
    H2N─C───C═O
            │
            OH

    ^^^^^   ^^^^^
    amino   carboxyl
    end     end
```

Two amino acids join by the amino end of one bonding to the carboxyl end of the other, kicking
out a water molecule. That link is a **peptide bond**, and a chain of them is a peptide.

Two consequences worth remembering:

1. **Direction matters.** A peptide runs from the **N-terminus** to the **C-terminus**. `GLFDII`
   and `IIDFLG` are different molecules, exactly as `dog` and `god` are different words. Order
   is information.
2. **The backbone is boring; the side chains are the story.** The `-N-C-C-` spine is identical
   everywhere. All the chemistry (charge, greasiness, size, ability to form bonds) lives in the
   twenty **R** groups.

So when we build features for machine learning, we are almost always summarising **side-chain
properties along a sequence**.

---

## 4. Five properties that explain most peptide behaviour 🧪

You don't need to memorise twenty amino acids. You need to know the handful of *axes* they vary
along.

| Property | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Charge** | positive, negative, or neutral at body pH | `K`, `R`, `H` are + · `D`, `E` are − |
| **Hydrophobicity** | does it prefer oil or water? | `L`, `I`, `V`, `F`, `W` hate water |
| **Size** | how much room the side chain takes | `G` is tiny · `W` is huge |
| **Polarity** | can it form hydrogen bonds? | `S`, `T`, `N`, `Q` are polar |
| **Special shapes** | structural oddballs | `P` kinks the chain · `C` forms disulfide bridges |

Now look back at our example, `GLFDIIKKIAESF`, with those axes in mind:

```python
SEQ = "GLFDIIKKIAESF"

HYDROPHOBIC = set("AVLIMFWYC")
POSITIVE    = set("KRH")
NEGATIVE    = set("DE")

n = len(SEQ)
print(f"length            : {n}")
print(f"hydrophobic ratio : {sum(c in HYDROPHOBIC for c in SEQ)/n:.2f}")
print(f"net charge        : {sum(c in POSITIVE for c in SEQ) - sum(c in NEGATIVE for c in SEQ):+d}")
```

```
length            : 13
hydrophobic ratio : 0.62
net charge        : +0
```

**62% greasy, net-neutral, 13 residues long.** That profile of short, half-oily and mixed
charges is the classic signature of a membrane-interacting peptide. Many antimicrobial peptides
look exactly like this: they carry a greasy face that slides into a bacterial membrane and a charged
face that grips its surface.

Three lines of Python, and we've already extracted something biologically meaningful. This is
why sequence-based ML on peptides works at all.

## 5. Sequence, structure, function: the chain of causation 🔗

The central dogma of this whole field:

$$
\text{sequence} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{structure} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{function}
$$

The letters determine the shape, and the shape determines what the molecule *does*.

A peptide's shape comes from a tug-of-war. Greasy side chains want to hide from water. Opposite
charges want to touch. The backbone can only bend certain ways. The molecule settles into
whatever arrangement makes all those pressures least unhappy. For peptides, that arrangement is
often only fully committed once the target is nearby.

```
   in water, floppy              at a membrane, folded
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~             ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
                                     ___
      G-L-F-D-I-I-K...             ⟋     ⟍     helix, greasy side down,
     (no fixed shape)             ⟍ ___ ⟋      charged side up
                                  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ membrane
```

For machine learning, the practical consequence is this: **if you skip the middle term, you
have to learn `sequence → function` directly.** That's harder, and it's exactly why honest
datasets matter so much. A model that never sees structure has nothing but statistical
regularities in the letters to go on. If your dataset has a shortcut, the model will find the
shortcut instead of the biology. That is the subject of a later post in this series, and the
reason my own peptide results read lower than the published numbers they sit beside.

## 6. What peptides actually *do* 💊

Peptides aren't a curiosity. They're a whole therapeutic class.

**Signals.** Insulin is a peptide hormone: 51 residues telling your cells to take in glucose.
Oxytocin is nine residues.

**Weapons.** Antimicrobial peptides punch holes in bacterial membranes. Because they attack the
membrane physically rather than a single enzyme, resistance is harder to evolve.

**Brakes.** **Anti-inflammatory peptides**, the family I work on, damp down an immune response
that has overshot. Inflammation is essential right up until it isn't; in rheumatoid arthritis,
inflammatory bowel disease, or a cytokine storm, the response itself is the disease.

**Drugs.** Peptide therapeutics sit in a genuinely useful middle ground:

| | Small molecules | Peptides | Antibodies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size | tiny | small | huge |
| Specificity | often loose | good | excellent |
| Oral dosing | usually yes | usually no | no |
| Manufacturing cost | low | medium | high |
| Off-target effects | more | fewer | fewest |

More specific than a small molecule, far cheaper and easier to make than an antibody. That
trade-off is why the field keeps growing.

## 7. Where machine learning comes in 🤖

Here's the problem in one sentence.

The space of possible 15-residue peptides has size

$$
20^{15} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{19}
$$

Thirty-three quintillion. Wet-lab testing one peptide costs real money and real days. Even at a
thousand tests a day you would not finish before the sun burns out.

So the question is not *"can we test them all"*. It's:

> **Given a sequence, can we predict whether it's worth testing?**

That's a classifier. Input: a string over a 20-letter alphabet. Output: a probability. And a
classifier that is even *moderately* good reshapes the economics entirely. Screen a hundred
thousand candidates in silico, send the top fifty to the bench.

The catch, and the thing that makes this interesting rather than routine, is that models eat
numbers and biology hands you letters. `GLFDIIKKIAESF` is not a feature vector. Turning it into
one, without throwing away the order, the charge pattern, or the local neighbourhoods that
matter, is the whole craft.

**That's the next post.**

---

## 8. The short version 📝

- Peptides are **short strings** over a 20-letter amino-acid alphabet, roughly 2 to 50 letters.
- **Direction matters**; the backbone is uniform and the **side chains** carry all the chemistry.
- Five axes explain most behaviour: **charge, hydrophobicity, size, polarity, special shapes**.
- `sequence → structure → function`. Skipping the middle term is what makes sequence-only ML
  both possible and fragile.
- The search space is astronomically large, which is precisely why prediction is worth doing.
